### Anomaly #1 (Most Interesting): Debt Ceiling Freeze -- Total Debt Flatlines for ~4 Months

**Period**: Approximately late April 2025 through early July 2025

During this stretch, total public debt outstanding barely moves, hovering in an extremely narrow band around $36,213-36,218 billion. For example:

| Date | Total Debt |
|------|-----------|
| 2025-06-30 | $36,211,469,351,821.31 |
| 2025-06-27 | $36,215,124,313,382.16 |
| 2025-06-20 | $36,215,873,428,835.86 |
| 2025-06-06 | $36,215,685,263,667.36 |
| 2025-05-15 | $36,216,429,412,019.81 |
| 2025-04-30 | $36,213,557,195,810.65 |
| 2025-04-01 | $36,215,156,673,113.80 |
| 2025-03-17 | $36,214,394,370,510.05 |

The total debt fluctuates by only ~$5 billion across roughly 4 months (March-June 2025), compared to normal daily swings of $20-60 billion. This is the classic signature of a **debt ceiling binding constraint** -- the Treasury hit the statutory borrowing limit and used extraordinary measures. The subcomponents (public debt and intragovernmental holdings) continued to move significantly day-to-day, but they offset each other to keep the total pinned near the ceiling.

**Why it's interesting**: This is the clearest debt ceiling episode in the dataset. The total is artificially frozen while subcomponents swing by $20-40 billion daily in opposite directions -- the Treasury was shuffling money between accounts under extraordinary measures.

| 2025-07-07 | $36,582,313,190,019.76 | **+$366.4 billion in one record gap** |

After the ceiling period, debt surged by ~$366 billion over a single weekend/holiday gap (July 4th weekend). This is one of the largest single-record jumps in the dataset. Public debt jumped from $29,030B to $29,217B (+$187B) and intragovernmental holdings jumped from $7,186B to $7,365B (+$180B). This represents the Treasury refilling its coffers after the debt ceiling was lifted, issuing a massive wave of new debt all at once.

**Why it's interesting**: The compressed release of pent-up borrowing creates one of the most dramatic discontinuities in the series.

### Anomaly #4: The 2023 Debt Ceiling Release -- $358 Billion in One Weekend

**Date**: 2023-06-02 to 2023-06-05

| Date | Total Debt |
|------|-----------|
| 2023-06-02 | $31,467,099,921,028.51 |
| 2023-06-05 | $31,825,678,823,229.44 |

Change: **+$358.6 billion** over a single weekend. This was the aftermath of the June 2023 debt ceiling resolution (Fiscal Responsibility Act). The dataset shows total debt frozen around ~$31,457-31,467 billion throughout January-May 2023 (the same ceiling pattern as Anomaly #1), then this explosive release.

Public debt jumped $24,702B to $25,032B (+$330B) while intragov went from $6,765B to $6,794B (+$29B). This is the single largest jump in the dataset and reflects the Treasury issuing ~$330 billion in new public debt in one burst to rebuild its cash balance.

**Why it's interesting**: This is the largest single-record increase in the entire 4-year dataset, and it marks the resolution of the 2023 debt ceiling crisis that nearly caused a US default.

### Anomaly #5: Intragovernmental Holdings Ratio Declining Over Time

Across the dataset, intragovernmental holdings as a share of total debt is declining:

- March 2022: ~21.4% ($6,496B of $30,282B)
- March 2023: ~21.2% ($7,286B of $36,214B -- but this is ceiling-distorted)
- October 2024: ~20.6% ($7,357B of $35,668B)
- March 2026: ~19.5% ($7,602B of $38,903B)

While the absolute value of intragov holdings grew from ~$6.5T to ~$7.6T (+$1.1T), total debt grew from ~$30.3T to ~$38.9T (+$8.6T). Nearly **87% of all new debt** was issued to the public (markets), not to government trust funds. The trust fund share of debt is structurally shrinking as the public-held portion grows much faster.

**Why it's interesting**: This structural shift means the government is increasingly dependent on market financing rather than borrowing from its own trust funds, which has implications for interest costs and market absorption capacity.

### Data Quality Issues

1. **Additive consistency**: The data appears clean -- total = public + intragov holds to the penny in all sampled rows.

2. **Weekend/holiday gaps**: The data correctly omits weekends and federal holidays (no Saturday/Sunday records). Gaps of 3-4 calendar days are normal (weekends plus holidays like MLK Day, Presidents Day, July 4th, etc.).

3. **No missing data**: The 1,000 records over ~4 years of business days is consistent (roughly 252 business days/year * 4 years = ~1,008 expected).

4. **Debt ceiling distortion**: During ceiling periods (Jan-Jun 2023 and Mar-Jul 2025), the total debt figures do not reflect true fiscal reality -- the Treasury was running down cash and using extraordinary measures. The headline number is artificially suppressed during these months.

5. **No negative total debt or obviously corrupt values**: All numbers are positive and in plausible ranges with monotonic overall growth trend, punctuated by the expected ceiling freezes and fiscal-year-end jumps.
